How the colors are chosen

This tool generates two-color combinations for an immersive projection room. A patient holds a soft controller — squeezing it increases the stimulation between the two colors. Releasing lets them converge back to a calm, almost uniform field.

The stimulation model follows Ruxandra Duru's framework: three properties control how stimulating a color pair feels — overall intensity, hue distance, and light-dark contrast. Push them up together and the pair becomes energizing. Pull them down and it becomes calming. The squeeze controller acts as a single fader for all three.

The temperature axis (controller tilt) shifts both colors through cool blues, neutral teals, and warm ambers. Both colors always stay related in temperature — they share a family, even as their stimulation diverges.

At zero stimulation, the two colors are nearly identical — the projection room becomes a pure color wash, like a James Turrell light installation. As stimulation increases, a reaction-diffusion pattern becomes visible between the two colors: organic, living channels and spots that the patient's gestures are shaping.

Color-emotion associations are drawn from a 30-country survey of 4,598 participants. Most colors map to positive emotions. Only brown, grey, and black are universally negative — the system avoids these entirely. Red is the most emotionally complex color (both love and anger). Pink has the strongest consensus for love and pleasure.
